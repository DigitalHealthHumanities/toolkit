As we finish this lesson, we want to stress some final thoughts that tie the ideas expressed earlier together. **We are not the end authority when it comes to the digital humanities or the digital health humanities**. Our arguments are not perfect, nor do they reflect the breadth of the digital humanities as a field. The ideas outlined in the previous section are the ones that give us a reason to continue to work with digital tools, however problematically we find that relationship.
	Max Liboiron and their colleagues have written about the ethics of working at specific scales, where we cannot reduce or eliminate our harms entirely. They write,

> our obligations and relations are often compromised, meaning we are beholden to some over others, and reproduce problematic parts of dominant frameworks while reproducing good relations at other scales. Compromise is not a mistake or a failure—it is the condition for action in a diverse field of relations.[^1]

For Liboiron and their colleagues, it is important to ground ethical interventions at specific scales, set in part by researchers and their **interlocutors**. We evoke this idea to be sensitive toward decisions that might lead researchers to adopt very different approaches to digital research. While we have outlined the critical digital humanities, it would be antithetical to the project of the digital humanities, with its myriad ‘what is DH?’ essays and its scholars working in a diverse and diffuse range of fields, to foreclose other approaches to digital technologies.

We framed the digital humanities and the digital health humanities in this specific way, because we want to be open about our own academic positionality. We have laid our cards out on the table because we do not want to assume our definitions to be read as neutral or monolithic, and because this positionality informs how we approached designing the lessons in this toolkit. Our hope, in developing teaching materials for new digital humanists, is to make the early phases of digital humanities research projects easier, and this involves teaching methods and theories, as well as teaching more nitty gritty skills related to project management.

## The Structure of this Toolkit

The Digital Health Humanities Toolkit is a collection of lessons developed to help onboard new digital humanists to skills related to the digital humanities and the health humanities. It is broken into four lesson groups:

- **Digital Humanities Crash Course (You Are Here)**: These lessons frame the toolkit’s broader learning goals, focusing on conceptual skills related to digital humanities theory.

- **[Digital Basics](ADD Link)**: A crash course in foundational aptitudes for working with digital methods. These lessons include introductions to methods in the digital humanities, ranging from tabular data structures, to textual analysis, to network graphs.

- **[Project Management](ADD Link)**: These lessons address the problems that occur when doing years-long digital research. Often humanists are not trained to think about projects at the scale of five or more years, nor do they learn how to bounce between multiple tools, program languages, and digital skills. These lessons help create the foundational skills that are required to smoothly prepare and execute a large digital project.

- **[Data Ethics](ADD Link)**: This final lesson group brings the health humanities into conversation with the digital humanities. Health information is especially sensitive, and it is important for scholars to be careful in how they approach their research. These lessons provide a framework for thinking about health data in ways that center the lives and afterlives of the subjects who are caught in the archive.
