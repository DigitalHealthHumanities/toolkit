So much of the data that makes computational systems work is in tabular form. From the metadata that makes databases run, to the data that makes our mapping programs work, to the metrics that guide our social media, all of this can be expressed in tabular form.

Tables have some limitations. They are Cartesian by design—everything can be mapped into an X and Y coordinate—and so they do not do well if they need to express more than those two dimensions. They also do very well at disciplining information—to borrow Michel Foucault’s famous framework around knowledge and prisons—because they assume all of the data can fit within the predefined metrics that correspond with the table’s construction.[^1] Moreover, they codify assumed pregivens—like gender or race—which are much slipperier in definition than data is usually able to represent.[^2]

These limitations cause friction when doing humanistic analysis: after all, as humanists, we tend to want to keep things messy and connected, embedded and subjective. Using a tool like Excel or any other digital spreadsheet software limits the stretchiness and malleability of our analyses, because they epistemically limit what is possible within analysis.[^3]

These problems are all over the digital humanities terrain, and cannot be answered in this lesson. We bring these up to stress a conceptual problem which digital humanists are working to address: digital tools offer an ability to process information at scales and within approaches that are very different than traditional humanist scholarship, but they are limited to the epistemic pregivens which are encoded into the very processes of doing digital work. These limitations are always at odds with the vibrant, varied possibilities enabled by these tools. Excel, lets us process large swaths of information, but only if we can get that information into the shape we need and if we can work with the protocols already developed for that program.

<div class=”backsplash”>Learning Excel helps us learn some of the fundamental preconditions for digital scholarship: Excel requires us to structure our data in such a manner that the computer will always look in the right place when we start to automate outputs. We ended with VLOOKUP because it is both powerful and finicky, and the kind of arbitrary ways that it functions is emblematic of the rigid ways programming languages operate. <strong>Computers need very specific rules to function, and they break when given unexpected inputs or outputs.</strong></div>

The rigidity of Excel and other programs is at odds with the fluidity and porosity of humanistic methods, but it can still be useful at different scales and for different projects. It sets up some epistemic problems, but it can still help do research at different scales and with different outputs.
