We have included this lesson on metadata into the toolkit for two practical reasons:

1. It is an important conceptual skill to have when working with digital files. How are files arranged? How do librarians and archivists structure their data? How can I find what I am looking for? 
2. When you start generating your own data, you have to think very critically about what matters and what does not. Each data point you want to create can equate to hours or days of additional processing time. Sometimes you do not have to do the labor of entering the data, because the metadata exists and can be computationally extracted, and sometimes you will have to do the work by hand.

More than these two more concrete reasons, we wanted to highlight metadata as a way to better understand how data is created and what principles guide that process of creation. Metadata, as a concept, gives off an appearance of objectivity, but often metadata is the result of subjective choices made by humans and their various motives, beliefs, opinions, and training in professional disciplines. More than this, metadata exists because of the affordances of external digital workflows and technologies, as well as the ideas that undergird those technologies. It plays into a system of understanding which, often, does not engage with our lived, embodied reality, but instead the mediated digital systems that we engage with to help streamline our research. Our decisions, when it comes to structuring our data and metadata, play into our own research objectives, the structures of the technologies we use, and the organizational systems we use or borrow from in the process.
