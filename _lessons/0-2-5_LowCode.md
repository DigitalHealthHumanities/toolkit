## Step 1: Draw a Map

Now we will get more hands-on with our activity. For this part, it may be easier to get a new sheet of paper, and we will return to our primary source. Again, we will not use any technical tools, so please do not consult any software, digital tools, or search engines like Google. We will base this activity on the information contained in Lucille Cowley Nixon’s oral history and our general knowledge of the United States. 
 
Set a 15-minute timer; you can use less time, but try not to use more. 

Using the primary source we just read, please draw a geographical representation (a map) of the places Lucille mentions in her oral history. Use the primary source to explore how people, places, and things interact. Please do not use any external tools. 

## Step 2: Reflection

Now that you have a hand-drawn map, let’s reflect on the activity. 

- What was your process for drawing your map? 
- What was most challenging about the activity? 
- What did you learn in the process?

When doing this activity in person, we typically place participants in groups so they can work together as challenges arise. One of the most common challenges with this activity is unfamiliarity with the geography of the places mentioned in the primary sources. The workshop participants have to work together and reach a consensus on where to place locations on the map using the information they have at their disposal, without using a search engine to pinpoint a location. Another challenge is deciding how to represent the places in the primary source. Sometimes, the locations are straightforward, as in the example we used, but with other primary sources, participants have to map battlegrounds using obscure location data, like "left of the river." When location data is more obscure, you may have to negotiate how to visualize the information for your audience and base it around your own interpretation of the data. This is the human component of digital humanities research.
