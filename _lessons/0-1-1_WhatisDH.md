Often for scholars curious about the digital humanities (DH) will hit an early roadblock around their perceived technological aptitude or skillset. They may tell themselves, “I do not know ArcGIS” or “I never learned to write in Python” or “I was never good at math in school” and shy away from the field’s computational approaches. Certain DH methods can be technically daunting, and the humanities has long been a home for those who refuse, critique, or avoid the kind of procedural and deterministic logics that guide the hard and soft sciences. For many, the adoption of the ‘digital’ goes against the basic principles which guide the humanities. 

We will go into some of these debates later in this lesson. What matters for this introduction is an acknowledgement that humanities scholars are not regularly trained in the {% include term.html term="methodologies" %} adopted by DHers, and as a result of this curricular gap, they may lack a baseline knowledge in how digital technologies operate. This lesson, and the broader [Digital Humanities Crash Course lessons]({{ site.baseurl }}/lessons/intro/) in this toolkit, will teach basic principles that are widely applicable for digital methods. Our goal for these lessons is to keep humanistic principles—those of {% include term.html term="subjectivity" %}, {% include term.html term="hermeneutics" %}, and the politics of texts—in the foreground, showing how the adoption of digital tools reveals tense, often problematic, assumptions for humanists and digital technologists alike.

{% include definition.html term="Methodologies" definition="Methodology refers to the study of practices and processes by which knowledge is obtained, described, or revealed. Every method has its own set of advantages and disadvantages when it comes to making a knowledge claim." %} 

{% include definition.html term="Subjectivity" definition="Subjectivity refers to the ways individual experience, positionality, or politics can inform an argument. Subjectivity is often described in opposition to 'objectivity', or the absence of politics, as it is used in the sciences." %} 

{% include definition.html term="Hermeneutics" definition="Hermeneutics refers to the philosophical field that describes the process of reading and understanding texts." %} 

We want to stress that **the digital humanities is for everyone**. What is important for how we (the authors of this lesson and most of the authors across this toolkit) imagine the digital humanities broadly is that is it founded on the humanistic critique of computational methodologies, and that while many DH projects use, adapt, and change these methods, at their core is a subjective, **socially constructed**[^1] knowledge framework.[^2]

{% include definition.html term="Social Constructivism" definition="Social constructivism refers to a philosophical tradition that sees that reality is understood, defined, experienced from social, historical, and political influences.  A post-war framework indebted to structuralism and post-structuralism, scholars use social constructivism in their arguments to push against deterministic arguments around sex and gender, about the adoption and use of technologies, and cultural practices. Most simply, social constructivism gives a framework to show how politics infuses social experience and knowledge claims." %}

This critique is technologically agnostic. You do not need a supercomputer to do digital humanities. You do not need to code to do digital humanities. You do not need expensive programs, teams of specialists, or hundreds of thousands of dollars in grant funding to do digital humanities.[^3] **What unifies most of the digital humanities is an interest in different methods afforded by the digital, a desire to create unique scholarship in our home disciplines, and a belief that the humanities can productively critique contemporary digital technologies and infrastructures.**

A theme of this lesson is an interrogation of humanistic and digital {% include term.html term="epistemology" %}*.[^1] This toolkit is built on the premises of social constructivism, and a view of digital technologies that is informed by science and technology studies (STS). Most simply, we advocate for humanists to learn digital humanities because the hands-on work helps humanists look under the hood of digital interfaces, what Julia Flanders has described as the digital humanities’ “productive unease.”[^4] 

{% include definition.html term="Epistemology" definition="Epistemology is the philosophical study of knowledge. It is interested in how and why knowledge is accepted, proved, or disproved. The lessons in this toolkit are informed by social constructivist conceptions of epistemology, which sees knowledge as dependent on a culture’s social, political, and technological infrastructures." %} 

With this in mind the next lessons will address some important concepts in the digital humanities. The next two sections will describe how we define the digital humanities, critical DH, and the digital health humanities. The section following that will describe data, and humanistic approaches to data, which inform the scholarship we seek to support. This lesson will incorporate no and low code digital methods—technologies and tools that can be picked up and used with little to no digital training or familiarity with coding languages.

Following these conceptual sessions will be some hands-on activities, to help demonstrate some of the concepts outlined in the more theory heavy sections.
