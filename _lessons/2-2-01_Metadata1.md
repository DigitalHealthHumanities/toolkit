In this lesson, you will learn about the concept of {% include term.html term="metadata" %}, and learn how it applies to digital research. Metadata, most simply, refers to data that describes other data, and it is used to help structure, organize, and computationally analyze data materials. We will talk about metadata from the perspective of a digital archivist, because it can help us learn how this concept can be applied to make our data structures easier to use and share with our colleagues.[^1]

{% include definition.html term="Metadata" definition="Metadata is data that describes other data. It can be used to help organize the original data, and/or it can be used to understand the data more fully through computational analysis." %} 

Metadata can be many things. Here are some examples:

- Creator(s) Names
- Publishing Date
- File Format
- Keywords
- Last Accessed Date
- Provenance Information
- Medium of Original Document
- Publisher
- File Size

These are far from exhaustive, but you may notice an important element about metadata: it describes the data, but it does not reflect the data fully. You may be working with a set of novels written by a single author, and while some of the metadata may be helpful--like the original publisher or the order in which the books are written--the book's actual data--the words written on the page--is probably more important. Other times, the descriptive information found in metadata can be helpful, as you are trying to find books in a single subject area, or track down materials with a similar medium.

## Digital Affordances

Digital objects are quite different than the material objects that we usually work with when we visit an archive. Think about a digital file as compared to a physical book: What are some of the affordances—-the unique properties of a given technology or medium—that you would use to describe each of these objects?

For a book, we may think in terms of its uniqueness, materiality, and physicality. We can pick it up. We can flip through its pages. We may have a special connection to a well worn copy of our favorite novel, with its unique bends in its paperback cover. And, for us bibliophiles, we may have to be very careful about which books we buy and how we organize our shelves, because of how much space they can take on the shelves.

Digital objects are much different: they are (almost) infinitely reproducible, and because of this, they can be accessed and shared widely. You may have a .pdf scan of an article that was shared with you in a seminar class, which the teacher acquired from a colleague who originally scanned the article for a class a decade prior. Digital files also do not take up much space—-either in the form of disk space, or in the material shape of a hard drive that can hold terabytes of data-—meaning hundreds, thousands, or even millions of files can be held together in a single collection.

Because of this reproducibility and scalability we have to be careful about how we structure our data, we may lose it. Have you ever lost a file because you named it “Paper_FinalversionFinalFinal.docx”? Have you ever lost a document because it was put in the wrong folder in a file cabinet? Now consider the file cabinet can hold millions of documents. How will you name your files in this context? How will you organize them?

Metadata helps us create organizational categories, sometimes following a standardized framework and sometimes built on out of our project’s needs. It does this by creating schemas and categories that can be used to structure the data. We can use a metadata structure to order our documents by date, isolate only a certain media type in a search, or exclude an author from our computational analysis.

This lesson will help you think critically about metadata to help you think through how to organize and structure the data in your research projects.
