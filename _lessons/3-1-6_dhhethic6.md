**This current lesson is an outline. There are elements that still need to be completed/fleshed out.**

Scholars often need to adopt a position of knowing everything possible, and making the correct decisions about, for example, a freshman writing assignment. Your Digital Health Health Humanities projects engages with and impacts the lives of other people in ways that you yourself do not and cannot fully understand. At some point in the course of developing or sharing your project, someone may raise objections to what you are doing. The objections may not be based on anything flagrantly illegal or against common practices in the field, but if your work is causing any level of harm, that matters. And responding from a defensive stance—“I tried to reach out to you but you didn’t reply”—does not address the harm. At the end of the day, you are making work that supports your career, off of their life or the lives of their loved ones. And if they express displeasure about your work, or indicate changes that must be made, you need to listen to them. 

This is time-consuming work, and energy draining. To be best prepared and positioned to do it, the relationships and understandings that you developed in Lessons 2-4 will support you in responding to concerns both from within the communities you have already identified and engaged with, and from outside of those communities. 

Humility is essential.

## Follow Through

In the stress and relief of finishing your work, don’t forget to follow-through on commitments you made. If a community group asked to share a link to the project in their newsletter, be sure to send it to them. If someone needed assistance being walked through the project on a computer, provide that. If a community archive requested hardcopies of all of the research material you gathered, share it (or, what you can ethically share).
