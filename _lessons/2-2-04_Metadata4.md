**For this activity, we will prompt you to write down some ideas on a piece of paper or on a document in a word processor.**

When developing any data structure, be it metadata or a dataset, you will want to be careful about **how you describe your materials** and **why you describe it in such a way**.

Let’s look at two objects from UCSF’s HIV/AIDS collections.

What kinds of structured information might you be able to generate from these two material objects? Brainstorm two lists on a separate piece of paper.

1. List out every piece of metadata information that you can think of that pertains to each object.
2. List out any descriptors or information that you might find useful about these materials that do not fit within your idea of metadata.

Your answers may have looked very different from ours. That is perfectly okay!

What you have to understand is that the way you approach the data might be different than your colleague’s approach. How you approach the data may be informed by your own background, bias, or discipline. There is no perfect approach; however, there can be approaches that help you manage your data or create arguments using that data.

When you do data entry, and when you structure your metadata, you will have an opportunity to make a bunch of decisions (often very quickly) about **what matters in your research**. Do you care about Barbara Bush? Do you care about bowling? Do you care about HIV/AIDS activism?

When in doubt about these decisions, it is best to return to your [research questions]({{ site.baseurl }}toolkit/lessons/intro/DHMethods/1) to help guide you. If you are working on a project about humor in HIV/AIDS activism, you may want to flag these archival objects as having humor incorporated in them, but you may not want to include that they are both yellow.

There is, technically, no wrong way to go about generating metadata or structuring your data, but it is worth remembering that every data point you track—-from the year a document was published, to the descriptive elements you want to follow—-will increase the time it takes to review and catalogue each object. If you are working with a few dozen objects, going into fine detail can be very valuable; however, if your objects number in the hundreds, thousands, or millions, each added detail can add hours or days to the processing time.
