Often when talking about the digital humanities and its importance in a given field, DHers will make an argument built on an assumption that the benefits move in one direction: we can apply digital methods to embrace new technologies and interdisciplinary imaginaries,[^1] and we can create new, novel forms of digital argument.[^2] This DH model sees digital methodologies as apolitical tools, and tends to see the digital in a techno-progressivist light. **Critical digital humanities**, as we are defining and using the term, sees itself as in conversation with the cultures and ideas that enable, extend, and develop digital and computational technologies. Working with the digital tools enables humanists to be intimately familiar with the **epistemic**, assumptions that enable those tools to work. This work allows digital humanists to investigate these frameworks from an uneasy ground,[^3] connecting those assumptions to longer histories of harm and the ideas about knowledge which enable those histories to continue in the present. More simply, digital humanities offers a space for humanists to productively critique digital technologies from a humanistic point of view.

{% include definition.html term="Critical Digital Humanities" definition="Critical digital humanities sees humanistic engagements with digital technologies as an opportunity to inspect and critique the cultural, ideological, and epistemic assumptions that ground those tools. It enables scholars to create models of computation that refute, refuse, or trouble the harmful frameworks that guide digital infrastructures." %}

Joanna Drucker, in her essay "Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display" outlines how humanist approaches embrace ambiguity, and how this is often at odds with the rigid, bounded ways data is calculated and displayed. Drucker defines a humanistic corollary to data—{% include term.html term="capta" %}, or a mode of measurement that sees the act of capture as a moment of interpretation. 

{% include definition.html term="Capta" definition="Capta is a reconfiguration of the pieces of information that are used in digital tools, seeing these materials as constructed, captured, and reflective of the cultures that create and use it." %}

If data purports to be an objective measurement of natural phenomena, capta, drawing on social constructivist approaches to knowledge-power, interrogates the assumptions made to make a measurement. Drucker writes, "Humanistic inquiry acknowledges the situated, partial, and constitutive character of knowledge production, the recognition that knowledge is constructed, taken, not simply given as a natural representation of pre-existing fact." [^4] More simply, capta asks what assumptions are made when collecting quantitative information. If we are doing research on sex and gender, how do we cordon off categories of male, female, intersex, or genderqueer experience? If we are doing political research, how do we define left or right leaning positions and what do we do about overlap or fuzziness in certain political ideas? For Drucker, humanist research muddies and problematizes quantitative, statistical, and computational practices. Describing a humanistic graph, developed to show the constructedness of *capta*, Drucker writes:

> any point or mark used as a specific node in a humanistic graph is assumed to have many dimensions to it – each of which complicates its identity by suggesting the embedded-ness of its existence in a system of co-dependent relations. Information entities, or units, are thus understood as fictional abstractions serving a purpose. But their potential to be read again in relation to any number of other equally significant relations can be made evident. This approach destroys the ground on which standard metrics are used to abstract quantitative information from human circumstances. Humanistic premises replace notions of statistical concepts of self-identity with entangled co-dependence and contingencies.[^4]

We go into a long description of Drucker’s argument to show the value of humanistic inquiry in digital contexts. The humanities provides the potential for an **epistemic** critique of the technologies scholars adopt in their research. More simply, it allows humanists to take apart digital tools and make arguments about the assumptions that guide their operation and use.[^5]

Critical DH is not monolithic, and it is not universally accepted by DHers. There are arguments to be had about the ways digital tools help research culture at scale,[^6] how DH projects can leverage technology to serve specific communities,[^7] and how DH can open doors and build connection.[^8] Those projects do not need a knowledge-power analysis of GitHub, or Mallet, or ArcGIS to function. Also, critical DH has incorrectly assumed that technologists are not savvy about issues of culture in their practice,[^9] and the field can ignore structural problems regarding funding, expertise, and accessibility.[^10] Moreover, cultural critique in many disciplines oftentimes does little to actually address the material, systemic, and historical problems it describes and analyzes.[^11] 

Central to our definition of critical DH is an assumption that our digital tools produce, reinforce, and extend existing hierarchies in our contemporary cultures. The concept of **data**—-a measurement reduced to a single point which corresponds to an assumed generalized structure that can be accessed and analyzed at scale-—has a longer history than Alan Turing, the original Macintosh, or the invention of the internet. Jessica Marie Johnson, looking to the practices that reduced slaves in the Transatlantic slave trade to a collection of data points (heigh, weight, sex), has shown that this quantification is part of the transformation that made human beings into commodities. She writes, "[t]here is no bloodless data in slavery’s archive. Data is the evidence of terror, and the idea of data as fundamental and objective information . . . obscures rather than reveals the scene of the crime."[^12]

{% include definition.html term="Data" definition="Data is the foundational unit for many forms of computational and non-computational research. Data, as we use the term, refers to the reduction of a phenomenon to a single measurement, allowing it to be placed within a structure that allows analysis to be employed at scale." %}

Kim Gallon describes this problem in her essay on Black digital humanities. She writes,

> One of the essential features of the black digital humanities, then, is that it conceptualizes a relationship between blackness and the digital where black people’s humanity is *not* a given. The black digital humanities probes and disrupts the ontological notions that would have us accept humanity as a fixed category, an assumption that unproblematically emanates in the digital realm. The black digital humanities, then, might be defined as a digital episteme of humanity that is less tool-oriented and more invested in anatomizing the digital as both progenitor of and host to new—-albeit related—-forms of racialization. These forms at once attempt to abolish and to fortify a taxonomy of humanity predicated on racial hierarchies.[^13]

The Black digital humanities assumes that digital technologies reproduce and reinforce the same ideas that enabled slavery, the Jim Crow, and the entrenched racial politics that are experienced by Black people in the United States and elsewhere. The digital’s assumed epistemic superiority, and data’s inexorable connection to sciences that rely on prediction, silently reproduce a totalitarian relationship between digital technologies and their users.[^14] It assumes people can be controlled (influenced through advertising); that the information about their lives can be bought and sold, collected through omnipresent surveilling practices; and that these methods are the best ways to produce a better utopian future. 

Miriam Posner, in a talk given to the Keystone Digital Humanities Conference in July 2015, gives a helpful framework for thinking about critical DH and how it informs critical scholarship that pushes against these assumptions. She writes, "[i]t’s not only about shifting the focus of projects so that they feature marginalized communities more prominently; it’s about ripping apart and rebuilding the machinery of the archive and database so that it doesn’t reproduce the logic that got us here in the first place."[^5]

Posner’s thinking comes from a reflection on what DH curriculum teaches students: Do we teach them to easily use technologies for their professional careers? Do we teach them how to close read those tools? Do we present clean studies or messy problems? Posner admits her own failing in this regard, but one in which tensions can be examined and interventions built. Critical DH, as we see the term, tries to keep its problems and provocations in view at the same time, and builds, through a {% include term.html term="speculative methodology" %},[^15] projects to analyze and undermine the problems it sees. Posner writes,

>We can do what we know how to do: visualize datasets that we inherit from governments and cultural heritage institutions, using tools that we’ve borrowed from corporations. Or we can scrutinize data, rip it apart, rebuild it, reimagine it, and perhaps build something entirely different and weirder and more ambitious.[^5]

{% include definition.html term="Speculative Metodology" definition="Speculative methods refers to approaches to culture and history that work to reinvent new models and approaches to violent pasts. Speculative historians work to create narratives that show the obvious gaps in the archive caused by carceral, colonial, white supremacist pasts, and other speculative methods imagine new ways of working against these same systems through the creation of new, imperfect methods." %}

The goal of critical DH is to take social constructivist, humanistic, and subjective approaches and build them into our tools and scholarship. The scale and practice of that work can be defined in different ways, depending on project goals, institutional resources,[10] and the needs of the communities scholars work to serve.[^16]
