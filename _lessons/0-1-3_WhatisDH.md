Our intent is to keep our view of DH broad, and so instead of hammering down a strict definition, we instead want to describe the DH research as sharing one or more interests and tensions, often examined through the use of a digital methodology—like computational textual analysis, GIS mapping, or the creation of a digital archive or repository.


### Research Interests in the Digital Humanities
- The affordances and possibilities of digital methods
- An interest in subjectivity
- Open publishing and open education
- Digital archives and repositories
- Interdisciplinary conversations
- Design-Based and Practice-Based Scholarship

### Tensions in the Digital Humanities
- Technological Skill versus Theoretical Interest
- Data’s Objectivity
- Ecological Impacts of Computing
- Access to Technology versus Computational Power
- Authority of Digital Tools in Research Arguments
- Implicit Bias in Technologies and Platforms

A main theme, for DHers and humanists more generally, is a negotiation between the affordances of digital tools, the authority of using logics of scale, and their implicit disconnect between the humanist project. More simply, the anxiety that drives critiques of DH is rooted in the ways digital research can displace the practice of reading and undermine subjectivity. If we were to take all of Shakespeare’s plays and run them through a computational textual analysis program, like AntConc or Voyant, having the computer count words and analyze them in relation to other words, would we have actually read the plays? Would we have understood their meaning? Are our findings relevant in any way to the centuries of scholarship around the Bard’s work? Did we have to build something to do this analysis, and how can we be sure that our new tool does what we say it does?

This last question points to another theme, that was especially precient in the aughts and teens. DH scholars advocated for revisions to tenure review practices to incorporate and normalize non-traditional, design-based scholarship. Rather than *only* accepting book-length projects or peer-reviewed essays as qualifying for promotion, DH scholars pushed to include the development of digital tools, the creation of digital archives and repositories, and work published on platforms like ArcGIS’ StoryMaps as being legitimate knowledge work. These arguments stressed the need for academic institutions to see practice as a kind of generative, theoretical mode of producing scholarship.

This problem is not one that we can address in full detail in this lesson, as it quickly becomes entangled in debates regarding the value and prominence of humanistic arguments. Our goal is not to undermine researchers who refuse DH, as there are valid reasons to push against techno-progressivist arguments in academic settings,[^*] and if DH is for everyone then it would be hypocritical to exclude our colleagues. Instead, we invite the DH curious to think about DH research through different approaches: complementary and additive DH, generative DH, critical DH, practiced and practical DH, and exploratory DH.

**Complementary and Additive DH** – Complementary and additive DH refers to the ways a digital method can be used to support a humanistic argument that relies on both digital methods and traditional methods like close reading. The digital method is used as a support, helping bolster the argument, but the claims would still be valid without digital analysis.

**Generative DH** – Generative DH refers to research that can only be done using digital tools. This could include a large-scale computational textual analysis of thousands of books, a detailed network graph with hundreds of nodes, or observations gleaned through statistical analysis of cultural materials.

**Critical DH** – Critical DH sees the use of digital technologies by humanists as an opportunity to interrogate the implicit logic which informs digital research and practice in general. If the first two approaches ask “what can digital methods do for my research?” critical DH asks “what can the humanities do to computational methods?”

**Practiced and Practical DH** – Practiced and practical DH refers to the design-based and practiced-based approaches DHers take to develop tools for humanistic research or to create non-traditional academic outputs.[^1] This approach sees DH as an opportunity to develop understandings of digital technologies through hands-on experimentation. 

**Exploratory DH** – Exploratory DH is an intentional catch all for those scholars who come to DH without an idea of what best suits them. This approach refers to early-phase examinations of how DH might be useful for a scholar, a student, or a team of researchers. It creates a space for a DH curious scholar to play and, possibly, reject digital methods in their research.

None of these approaches need to be seen as monolithic, and often times during research scholars will adjust how they see their project, and how they use DH, as they reflect on their findings. DH can be additive, or transformative, or critical, or practical, or not useful at all.

While we are working to be inclusive in our description of DH as a field and practice, we would be dishonest if we did not show our own bias in how it informs our approach to DH and the digital health humanities. We write from the perspective of critical DH, a field that sees humanistic inquiry into digital technologies as a necessary intervention against the harmful, dehumanizing, and oppressive logics that guide digital technologies and infrastructures. In the next section, we will outline this approach more concretely.
