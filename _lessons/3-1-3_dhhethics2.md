Are you a member of one of your audiences? What are the power relations at play? 

A good place to start, if you haven’t already approached these questions, is with a consideration of your positionality.

Where there is a power imbalance; slow down and think about ethics. 
 
How might the difference between you and your subject cause issues in your research? How might you address these biases or potential harms?

> One area of privilege and power imbalance that will apply to most Digital Health Humanities Projects is that arising when a person who is part of an academic institutional hierarchy engages with a community that is either inside or outside of the academy. First, it’s essential to see the unequal power relations at play here. The needs of institutions and of communities are inherently incompatible.

Some resources to learn about this: 
          	Ebony and Ivy
          	Museums are not neutral

Central to all of this is capitalism’s chrononormativity: Universities have semesters, Museums have Fiscal Years, grants have dates they must start and end. Real life does not proceed on these timetables; and perhaps even moreso, the relationship building required to make work that is sensitive to community make take longer, depending on the nature of the harms that community has suffered. 

With that in mind, it’s really important to slow down and proceed with care when we want to create work that supports community needs. We’ll talk more about how to identify your community’s needs in the next lesson. 
But even knowing nothing about your specific community, what does your community not need?
- Condescending assumptions, microaggressions
- Expectation of uncompensated labor: meetings, interviews
- Emotional labor your project asks of them: learning about or revisiting community trauma
