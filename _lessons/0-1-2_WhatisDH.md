There are as many definitions of the digital humanities (DH) as there are DH practitioners. The question, “What is DH?”, is a meme in the community, drawn from a flurry of boundary setting essays and blog posts written during the field’s popular emergence in the early twenty-first century.[^1] The field has a history that long predates this period, and the arguments and methods humanities computing scholars developed inform the methods contemporary DHers still use.

For this section, our interest is not in retreading these conversations. As we wrote in the introduction, **the digital humanities is for everyone**. To support this claim requires a very loose disciplinary definition. We will admit that this openness has its limitations, but our choice in this framing is intentional. STEM disciplines have historically used gatekeeping practices—-weed out courses, hyper specialized degrees, and an expectation of unpaid labor to gain experience—-to maintain an intellectual cachè, and the humanities in post-secondary education have been a landing ground for students who bounced off the barriers to these degree programs. More than this, humanists often feel reticence about their aptitude to even learn basic digital methods, because of their past histories, because of the humanities’ sometimes antagonistic stance against {% include term.html term="positivistic" %} or quantitative research, and because their schooling did not incorporate digital tools in the curriculum. A broad definition of DH is an invitation to bring those who see themselves as nontechnical and to see their experience as additive to the DH project.

{% include definition.html term="Positivism" definition="Positivism refers to a philosophical viewpoint where all phenomena can be described outside of human cultural influence. Positivism is often positioned against social constructivism, and it is especially interested in the prediction of natural phenomena." %} 

The longer histories of DH look back to developments in the twentieth century. Humanities computing, a field that was interested in using computation to analyze largely written materials with an emphasis on archives and literature, dates back to the 1960s.[^2] Narratives often cite the prominence of the University of Virginia’s English department in this early period, as faculty adopted early computational methods to digitize and examine texts.[^3] What these histories tend to prioritize is a linear progression tied largely to {% include term.html term="textual analysis" %} and English departments; it should be understood that humanities scholars—even scholars who do not claim to be digital humanists—have long adopted digital technologies to discuss, develop, and share their research, either through the use of digital word processors or through technologies like the listserv.[^4] 

{% include definition.html term="Textual Analysis" definition="Textual analysis refers to a humanistic methodology by which cultural materials--books, movies, advertising, architecture, and so on--can be examined for implicit, hidden, or intentional cultural signifiers." %} 

While there is merit to discuss the different technologies as they were adopted by humanists, a subtheme in DH scholarship has been its ability to successfully draw in grant funding. The National Endowment for the Humanities’ (NEH) Office of Digital Humanities opened in 2008, coincidently around the same time as the subprime mortgage collapse. The influx of grant funds, as well as the field’s ability to apply for grants from funders like the National Science Foundation (NSF), enabled DH’s growth in a period when every other humanities discipline was gutted. The austerity programs universities practiced to survive the 2008 financial collapse never went away, and DH has, for better and worse, been a source of research funding for humanists of all stripes.

We outline this broad history not to quibble over an essential character of what makes DH, but to link it to a longer series of practices and moments. DH is, for some, driven out of an interest in computing as a tool and practice. It is, for others, practices that connect and enable scholarship. And it is, for the most cynical of us, a way to advocate for funding in a resource strapped university environment. More than this, DH scholars hail from disciplines as diverse as history and anthropology, sociology and archaeology, and media studies and literature. The big tent, as it was called in the early teens, enabled scholars to share methodological and epistemic overlap with one another while still enabling those scholars to speak directly to their home disciplines.[^5] The fact that many DHers have a scholarly footing between the digital humanities and their home disciplines is both a boon—-enabling novel research—-and a limitation—-as some disciplines look at the supposedly new field with varying degrees of skepticism. What matters for us, and for this lesson, is that DH has become a loose community that is a home for some, a hostel for others, and crossroads for many more. 

Because of this flexibility, we view DH as a conversation, a collaboration, and a community in whatever form best suits the needs of individual scholars, institutions, and academic disciplines, and this scholarship is often engaged through an interest in culture, computation, and academic practice.
